A polyp is a small growth on the inner lining of the colon. Healthy lining is made of tidy tubes of cells that renew every few days. Polyps grow in two main shapes. Conventional polyps are raised, like a mushroom, and they are the common kind. Serrated polyps are flat, with saw-tooth folds, and are easy to miss during a colonoscopy. Most polyps stay harmless, but some turn into cancer over about ten years, by either route. The two pictures at the bottom are real stained tissue slices from our own data, one conventional and one serrated polyp, from two different people; the dashed line is the area a pathologist marked as polyp. The conventional one is a thick, crowded mass; the serrated one is a thin, flat strip with healthy lining in the middle. Why it matters: colorectal cancer is diagnosed about 1.9 million times a year and kills about 900,000 people (GLOBOCAN 2020). Most of these cancers start as a polyp, so finding and removing polyps prevents cancer. And up to 30 percent of colorectal cancers come by the serrated route, from the flat polyps that are hardest to see.
