To finish, four next steps, each built on what we already found. One, line up people. Right now polyp cells clump by person, even though healthy cells from different people mix fairly well. We want a model, for example a VAE, that learns to ignore whose cell it is. We call it a success only if healthy cells from different people mix and the conventional and serrated new cells still stay apart, because part of the person clumping may be real: each polyp grows from its own cells. Two, treat the polyp as the odd one out: learn what healthy lining looks like and score how far each polyp cell sits from it. This is easy to measure: train without one person, then check whether the score tells that person's polyp from their own lining, and whether the highest scores land on the new cell types. Our "no look-alike" cells are already a simple version of this score. Three, from healthy to polyp: learn a flow, like an ODE, that moves the healthy cells into each route's polyp, including the new cell types, which do not exist in healthy lining. Then, given someone's healthy cells, the model says what each route would look like. We test it by predicting a left-out person's polyp, and the one person who has both kinds of polyp is a direct check. One honest limit: our samples are snapshots, so the flow is a best guess at the change, not one polyp watched over time. Four, polyp in the tissue: use each person's single cells to fill in the blurry tissue dots and map how far the polyp spreads, including whether the healthy lining next to a polyp already carries part of its change. All six people behind our tissue slices also have single cells. Thank you.
