Mutation ladder: both routes

Cancer builds up step by step: each broken gene lets the polyp grow a little further.

Fearon & Vogelstein, Cell 1990

Conventional: starts in stem cells

Stem cells divide all the time to renew the lining; the first break jams their off switch, so they never stop.

Serrated: starts in mature cells

Mature cells near the surface should rest; a growth switch stuck on wakes them up, then many genes go silent.

What do current models tell us? The classic mutation ladder says cancer builds up step by step, as genes break one by one, and both routes climb a ladder like this; they differ in where the climb starts. In conventional polyps it starts in stem cells, the cells at the bottom of each tube that divide all the time to renew the lining. The first break jams their off switch, a growth brake gene, so they never stop dividing. In serrated polyps it starts in mature cells near the surface, which should have stopped dividing. A growth switch gets stuck on and wakes them up, and then many genes go silent. The stem-versus-mature picture comes from the atlas we use. Why is that not enough? First, these models describe the average polyp, pooled across many people, so each person’s own background blurs the picture. Second, the first mutation is not enough: about one percent of perfectly healthy colon tubes already carry cancer-gene mutations by middle age, and the serrated switch mutation also sits in harmless polyps. Third, none of them explains the person we just saw, with both routes in one colon. Eduardo asked us whether this needs new analysis tools. Our answer is yes: standard tools compare groups of people, and our question lives inside each person. (Fearon and Vogelstein, Cell 1990: APC, KRAS, TP53 ladder.
