Our data come from an open colon atlas, with two kinds of data. The main one is single-cell RNA, which reads which genes are switched on in each individual cell. Thirty-three people gave a polyp and a sample of their own healthy lining: 19 conventional and 15 serrated polyps, about 93 thousand lining cells. They come in two separate groups of people, 20 and 13, so we can find a result in one group and check it in the other. In total the atlas holds about 123 thousand lining cells from 55 people; we use the 93 thousand from people who gave both a polyp and healthy lining. The second kind is tissue slices, measured with 10x Visium: gene readings at mapped spots, with a photo of the tissue, for 7 polyps, about 6,900 spots in all. We use those to check that our route genes light up where the polyp is. Here is what made the question real for us. One person had two polyps in the same stretch of colon. A pathologist called one conventional and one serrated. The cells agree: the healthy lining has essentially none of the polyp-only cell types; the conventional polyp grew adenoma-type cells, 6 percent, and the serrated polyp grew serrated-type cells, 27 percent. Same inherited genes, same diet, same part of the colon, yet two routes. So the person’s background alone does not decide the route, and the clean way to see what a polyp changed is to compare it with the same person’s healthy tissue. (Donor HTA11_6801, VAL: conventional 65 of 1,014 cells ASC; serrated 342 of 1,264 SSC; two normal samples 1 SSC in 1,689 cells. Polyp 1 also carries 2% SSC, polyp 2 1% ASC. All 55 people and 122,811 epithelial cells including unpaired and cancer samples; paired people 53,292 + 39,779 cells. Visium: fresh-frozen, Spatial 3' v1, 55 micrometre spots, 6,915 tissue spots over 7 captures from 6 people, all 6 also in the single-cell data. Single-cell: TruDrop, inDrop-style droplets; 10,696 further non-epithelial cells exist but are not used.
